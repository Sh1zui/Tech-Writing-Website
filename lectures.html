1 Lecture 1 of 7

Technical Writing: Definition and Purpose

By the end of this lecture you can
  • define technical writing and list its main characteristics;
  • explain the three purposes of technical writing;
  • analyze audience, purpose and context before you write; and
  • describe the strengths and limits of modern communication channels.

What is technical writing?

Technical writing is writing that is characterized by certain formal elements: its scientific and technical vocabulary, its use of graphic aids, and its use of conventional report forms. In plain terms, it is the craft of explaining specialized information so that a particular reader can understand it, decide something, or act on it.

Because the reader depends on the text to do something correctly, a technical document must be clear first and attractive second. Think of a user manual, a lab report, a project proposal or a set of safety instructions: each one exists to help someone use the information.

Key idea

Technical writing uses technical vocabulary, tables, graphs and figures. Examples of technical terms: SDLC, HTML, Java, SQL, GDP, emulsification, morpheme, metastasis. Each belongs to a field whose readers know it.

Qualities of technical writing

Good technical writing is held to five standards. Each one protects the reader from confusion.

Table 1. The five qualities of technical writing
QualityWhat it meansExample
ImpartialTreats all sides fairly and does not take a side without evidence.“Both designs passed the test; Design B cost less.”
ObjectiveReports facts, not personal feelings or preferences.“The device weighs 1.2 kg.” (not “The device is annoyingly heavy.”)
AccurateData, names and calculations are correct and verifiable.“The pump runs at 45 liters per minute.”
ConciseUses only the words needed to deliver the message.“Restart the router.” (not “It would be a good idea to consider restarting the router.”)
UnemotionalKeeps a calm, professional tone, even when reporting problems.“The test failed at 80 °C.”

Technical writing compared with other writing

Table 2. Technical writing vs. general (literary) writing
FeatureTechnical writingGeneral / literary writing
Main goalInform, instruct or help the reader decideEntertain, express feelings, tell a story
ReaderA specific, defined audienceA broad or unspecified audience
LanguagePrecise, literal, standard terminologyFigurative, expressive, open to interpretation
ToneImpartial and unemotionalPersonal and emotional
StructureConventional forms with headings, lists, numbered stepsFlexible; chosen by the author
VisualsTables, graphs, diagrams, figuresIllustrations are optional

Why write? The purposes of technical writing

1. To inform

Gives readers facts they need. Example: a memo announcing the new enrollment schedule.

2. To persuade and influence

Convinces readers to approve or adopt something. Example: a proposal for a new computer laboratory.

3. To analyze events

Examines what happened and why. Example: an incident report on a network outage.

Factors to consider before writing

Experienced writers answer three questions before typing the first sentence: Who will read this? Why am I writing it? Under what conditions is it written and used?

A. Audience analysis

Consider the readers’ educational background, technical expertise, cultural background, professional context, and reading level. The same instruction must be written differently for different readers.

For developers

“To integrate the API, initialize the client using your secret bearer token and pass the payload via a POST request.”

For general users

“To connect the app, copy the password from your settings page and paste it into the box labeled ‘Secret Key’.”

Neither sentence is “wrong.” Each is right for its own audience, which is the point.

Too hard for a patient

“The patient exhibited acute symptoms of rhinitis secondary to environmental allergen exposure, necessitating the administration of an antihistamine.”

Plain language for a patient

“You have a cold caused by allergies. Take one allergy pill every morning with water.”

B. Purpose

A clear purpose has five parts. Each is shown below with a weak version and a stronger version.

Clear objective identification
Weak

“This document is about our company’s new cloud database software.”

Better

“This document provides step-by-step instructions for migrating legacy client data to our new cloud platform without system downtime.”

Measurable outcomes

State how success will be checked. Example: “A 30% reduction in customer-service tickets about initial router setup within the first three months of publication.”

Information hierarchy
Weak

A manual that puts code snippets, system requirements and setup instructions into one continuous block of paragraphs.

Better

A README that begins with a Title, then a Quick Start Guide, then a Full Installation Guide, and ends with an Advanced Configuration appendix.

Actionable content
Weak

“Consideration should be given to clicking the blue button if an error occurs, or maybe restarting the machine if the problem continues.”

Better

“If an error appears, click Retry. If the error persists, restart the machine.”

Value proposition for readers
Weak

“This manual details the standard update protocols for Version 4.2 of our software.”

Better

“By following this Version 4.2 upgrade guide, your team can automate daily server backups, saving up to five hours of manual administration every week.”

C. Context

Context covers the conditions around the document: industry standards, legal and regulatory rules, organizational guidelines, time limits, and available resources.

Industry standards and requirements
  • Engineering: a schematic must use standard IEEE symbols so any technician in the world can read the blueprint.
  • Software: API documentation follows OpenAPI standards so other software can parse the instructions automatically.
Legal and regulatory compliance
  • Medical devices: a heart-monitor manual must follow FDA guidelines and clearly show contraindications, side effects and “Warning” boxes.
  • Consumer electronics: a smartphone guide includes local radiation-safety notices (such as FCC rules in the US) and environmental disposal rules.
Organizational guidelines

A company can require specific fonts, tone and heading formats. Example: a Google technical writer follows Google’s own style and design guidelines, writing in a helpful, conversational yet professional tone.

Time constraints

A critical security flaw is found. The writer has only 24 hours to publish a security advisory and a three-step mitigation guide, so the document must be short, clear and ready fast.

Resource availability
  • High resources: a multinational car maker supplies 3D CAD models, a video crew and a budget to translate the manual into 40 languages.
  • Low resources: a small start-up has one writer using free tools, automated translation and brief 15-minute interviews with busy developers.

Problems of a writer

Historical challenges

  • Limited access to resources
  • Manual documentation processes
  • Standardization of technical language

Contemporary challenges

  • Information overload
  • Rapid technological change
  • Ensuring content authenticity
  • Declining literacy skills

Contemporary communication

Contemporary communication refers to modern methods and practices of exchanging information in organizations and society, influenced by technology, globalization and changing workplace dynamics. It emphasizes speed, efficiency, interactivity and adaptability.

Features

  • Technology-driven
  • Speed and timeliness
  • Interactivity
  • Global reach
  • Multichannel integration
  • Flexibility and adaptability
  • Real-time feedback and monitoring

Modern tools and channels

Instant messagingQuick questions and team coordinationKeep it short; save long topics for email.

Information overload · misinterpretation of messages · dependence on technology · reduced personal interaction · security and privacy concerns · distractions and reduced focus · digital divide and accessibility issues · over-reliance on written communication.

ABCs of Technical Writing

By the end of this lecture you can
  • name and explain the 26 “alphabet” qualities of good technical writing; and
  • use the ABCs as a checklist to review your own drafts.

The alphabet of technical writing

The ABCs are a memory aid: one quality for every letter. Use them as a checklist before you submit any report. Type in the box to filter the list.

Showing all 26 letters
  • A

    Accuracy

    Tactfulness in recording data; every fact and figure is correct.

  • B

    Brevity

  • C

  • D

  • E

  • M

    The general appearance of the report: layout, margins, formatting.

  • N

    Normal procedure

    Conformity to the general standard or accepted format.

  • O

    Objectivity

  • P

    Gives purpose and direction to the writing process.

  • Q

  • R

Grouping the ABCs

Twenty-six items are hard to remember at once. Group them by the question each one answers:

Is the content right?

Accuracy, Honesty, Judgment, Knowledge, Logic, Qualification, Thoroughness, Objectivity.

Is it easy to read?

Brevity, Emphasis, Facility, Straight sentences, Unity, Viewpoint, Word choice, You-point, Grammatical correctness.

Is it professionally presented?

Confidence, Dignity, Illustration, Mechanical neatness, Normal procedure, Planning, Revision, Extra effort, Zest.

The ABCs at work

Here is a first draft of an announcement, followed by a revision that applies several ABCs.

First draft

“So basically the thing with the lab is that it’s kinda closed for a while because some stuff is being fixed and it will probably open again soon-ish. Sorry guys!”

Revised

“The Computer Laboratory is closed from October 14 to 16 for network repairs. It will reopen on October 17 at 8:00 AM. We apologize for the inconvenience.”

  • Accuracy and thoroughness: exact dates, place and reason.
  • Brevity and word choice: removed filler (“basically,” “kinda,” “stuff”).
  • Dignity: a courteous apology instead of “guys.”
  • Emphasis: the closing dates appear first.

Outlining the Subject

By the end of this lecture you can
  • limit a broad topic to a manageable subject;
  • write a topic outline and a sentence outline using standard and decimal formats; and
  • develop a subject using definition, classification, partition, process description and mechanism description.

Limiting the subject

Limiting the subject means narrowing a broad topic to one specific aspect so the content is concise, relevant and manageable for the intended audience. It prevents overwhelming readers with unnecessary information.

To limit a subject: identify the core topics, set boundaries, eliminate unnecessary information, focus on essential elements, and keep everything relevant.

Worked example: narrowing with Who, What and How

Broad topicRemote work / freelancing
+ WHOFresh college graduates in the Philippines
+ WHATFinding their first freelance clients
+ HOWThrough online freelance marketplaces

Limited subject: How fresh Filipino college graduates can find their first freelance clients through online marketplaces.

Outlining the subject

An outline is a formal system for thinking about and organizing your paper. It uses Roman and Arabic numerals with capital and small letters to show the order of topics, the relative importance of each, and the relationship between parts. It is a framework that makes your document logical, focused and easy to follow.

Standard (alphanumeric) outline hierarchy

I. Roman numerals (main topics)
A. Capital letters (subtopics)
1. Arabic numbers (details)
a. Lowercase letters (sub-details)
(1) Parenthetical Arabic numbers
(a) Parenthetical lowercase letters

Decimal outline hierarchy

1.0 MAIN HEADING (broadest idea)
1.1 Sub-heading (supports 1.0)
1.1.1 Detail or example (supports 1.1)
1.1.1.1 Sub-detail
1.1.2 Detail or example
1.2 Sub-heading
2.0 MAIN HEADING (next main point)

Two kinds of outline

Topic outline

Short phrases. Useful when several issues could be arranged in different ways.

Sentence outline

Full sentences. Normally used when the paper deals with complex details.

How to write an outline

  1. Identify the main topic or purpose.
  2. Determine the main ideas or arguments.
  3. Put the ideas in order.
  4. Add supporting details.
  5. Review and revise.
Rules for outlines

Every level needs at least two items (if there is an “A,” there must be a “B”). Items at the same level should be parallel in form. Each item belongs under only one heading.

Developing the subject: five basic techniques

Once a subject is limited and outlined, writers develop each part with a technique that fits the reader’s need.

1. Definition

To define is to give the meaning of a term. There are three levels:

Table 4. Three kinds of definition
KindFormExample
InformalA word or phrase (a synonym)“Compensation” and “remuneration” can be simplified as pay. A smartphone is like a small computer.
FormalOne sentence with species, genus and differentiaA barometer is an instrument that measures atmospheric pressure.
AmplifiedA paragraph that expands a formal definitionSee the “leader” example below.
SpeciesA thermometer
Genusis an instrument
Differentiathat measures temperature.

Species is the term being defined; the genus is the class it belongs to; the differentia are the traits that separate it from others in that class. More examples: A somnambulist is a person who walks while asleep. A somniloquist is a person who talks while asleep.

Amplified definition example: “leader”

“A leader is not simply someone who tells others what to do; a leader is a visionary who inspires, motivates, and empowers a team to achieve a shared goal. A leader fosters a positive and collaborative environment, provides support and guidance, and delegates effectively. A leader also communicates well, makes difficult decisions, and adapts to changing circumstances. Above all, a leader leads by example, showing integrity and commitment to the team’s success.”

The first sentence acts as the topic sentence; every sentence after it develops the definition.

2. Classification

Classification arranges objects, people or ideas that share characteristics into classes or groups. Use one basis for sorting, and make sure the groups do not overlap.

Example. Vehicles, classified by the medium in which they travel:

  • Land vehicles: bicycles, jeepneys, trains
  • Water vehicles: bancas, ferries, cargo ships
  • Air vehicles: airplanes, helicopters, drones

3. Partition

Partition divides one single item into its parts, steps or aspects. The parts need not have anything in common except that they belong to the same unit.

Table 5. Classification compared with partition
ClassificationPartition
Works onMany items sorted into groupsOne unit divided into parts
Parts have in commonA shared characteristicOnly that they belong to the same unit
ExampleComputers: desktops, laptops, tabletsA laptop: screen, keyboard, processor, battery

4. Description of a process

A process description explains how an action happens. It is organized as: A. Introduction, B. Step-by-step description of the action, C. Conclusion (if necessary).

Example: submitting an assignment on an online learning platform

Introduction. Submitting an assignment online takes five steps and about three minutes. You need a file saved in PDF format and an internet connection.

  1. Log in to the learning platform with your student account.
  2. Open the course and select the assignment link.
  3. Click Add submission.
  4. Drag your PDF into the upload box and wait for the progress bar to finish.
  5. Click Save changes, then confirm the status reads “Submitted for grading.”

Conclusion. A confirmation message and a time stamp appear when the submission succeeds. Keep a screenshot as proof.

5. Description of a mechanism

A mechanism description explains a device’s physical components, its function, and how the components relate. It answers: “What is it, what are its parts, and what does it do?” It is essential for maintenance manuals, product specifications and user guides.

Example: a retractable ballpoint pen (see Figure 13 in Lecture 7)

What it is. A ballpoint pen is a hand-held writing instrument that deposits oil-based ink on paper through a rolling ball.

Parts. It has five main parts: the cap, the barrel, the spring, the ink cartridge and the ballpoint tip.

How they work together. The barrel holds the cartridge and the spring in line. The cartridge stores the ink and feeds it to the tip. At the tip, a tiny steel ball turns as the pen moves, picking up ink from inside and rolling it onto the paper. The spring returns the cartridge to its resting position, and the cap protects the tip when the pen is not in use.

Related technique

Comparison and contrast places two subjects side by side to show how they are alike (comparison) and how they differ (contrast). Organize it either subject by subject or point by point, and use the same points for both subjects.

Types of Technical Paper

By the end of this lecture you can
  • distinguish informal reports from formal reports and list their parts;
  • write the header of a memorandum and the parts of a letter report;
  • tell a descriptive abstract from an informative abstract; and
  • outline a proposal and choose the right paper for a given situation.

Technical reports

A technical report is a formal document that presents data, research or findings in a structured, objective and detailed way. It is a specific type of technical writing used to deliver specialized information to a specific audience.

General report layout

Table 6. General parts of a technical report
PartWhat it contains
Title pageTitle of the report, author’s name, date and the organization’s name.
Table of contentsThe sections and sub-sections with their page numbers.
IntroductionBackground information, the purpose of the report and the scope of the work.
BodyThe data, analysis and results, usually divided into sections with headings.
ConclusionsA summary of the key findings and outcomes.
RecommendationsA suggested course of action based on the findings.

Informal and formal reports

1. Informal report

A short report designed for circulation only within an organization. Common format: the title, the author’s name, an abstract (if any), and the text.

Kinds: memorandum report, letter report, bulletins, booklets, abstract.

2. Formal report

A structured, comprehensive document with formal elements such as a transmittal letter, an executive summary and detailed sections, written for external readers or high-level decision-makers.

Memorandum report

A memorandum (memo) report is an internal business document used for communicating within an organization. It has two main parts: the header and the body.

  • TO: the recipient’s name and title.
  • FROM: the sender’s name and title.
  • DATE: the date the memo is written.
  • SUBJECT: a concise, clear summary of the topic.
  • Body: the main text, often organized with headings or bullet points for easy reading.

Letter report

A letter report is a technical report written in the format of a business letter and used for external communication. Its parts: sender’s address; date; recipient’s address; salutation (such as “Dear Mr. Smith:”); the body, broken into paragraphs with clear topic sentences; a professional closing (“Sincerely,” or “Respectfully,”); and the signature with the sender’s name and title. Lecture 6 explains each part in detail.

Abstract

An abstract is a short, standalone summary of a larger work that helps readers quickly decide whether the full document is relevant to them. There are two types.

Descriptive abstract

States what the document contains. “This report discusses the causes of slow Wi-Fi in the school library and lists three possible solutions.”

Informative abstract

Summarizes the purpose, methods, results and conclusion. The most common type for technical reports. “This study measured Wi-Fi speed at 20 library seats over five days. Speeds fell below 2 Mbps during peak hours because 60 users shared one access point. Adding two access points is recommended.”

Proposal

A proposal is a persuasive document that outlines a plan or project and convinces a specific audience to approve it. Typical sections:

  1. Introduction: hook the reader; briefly state the problem and the solution.
  2. Problem statement: define the issue or need clearly.
  3. Proposed solution: describe your plan in detail.
  4. Methodology: the steps you will take to carry it out.
  5. Timeline: a schedule for the project.
  6. Budget: a breakdown of costs.
  7. Conclusion: a final, persuasive summary.

Sentence structure

Sentence structure is how the parts of a sentence are arranged: word order, clauses and punctuation. Every sentence has a subject + predicate. Our school team (subject) won the match (predicate).

Components of a sentence

Table 9. Sentence components
ComponentMeaningExample
Subject (S)Performs the actionThe child kept crying.
Verb (V)Shows the actionThe child kept crying.
Object (O)Noun or pronoun that receives the action. Direct object (DO): what or whom. Indirect object (IO): to or for whom.My mom made a cake (DO). My mom bought me (IO) a new dress (DO).
Complement (C)Words needed to complete the meaningThe students are very excited. The students elected Reya the leader.
Adjunct (A)Extra information that answers when, where, why, how, how often or to what extentYesterday, we met at the park.

Practice: identify the components

“The new manager appointed Sarah the team leader yesterday.”

S: The new manager · V: appointed · O: Sarah · C: the team leader · A: yesterday.

“The committee considered the proposal viable despite initial concerns.”

S: The committee · V: considered · O: the proposal · C: viable · A: despite initial concerns.

“The diligent student completed his challenging assignment successfully last night.”

S: The diligent student · V: completed · O: his challenging assignment · A: successfully, last night.

Sentence patterns

Table 10. Common sentence patterns
PatternExamples
Subject + Verb (SV)The stars are shining. The birds are chirping.
Subject + Verb + Object (SVO)Children love ice cream. Noah does not like bitter gourd.
Subject + Verb + Complement (SVC)Carol is a nurse. I am a Filipino.
Subject + Verb + Adjunct (SVA)All of us are leaving for Tokyo. He went upstairs.
Subject + Verb + Indirect Object + Direct Object (SVIODO)Rina gave Reshmi his favorite book. The Board offered me the job.
Adjunct + Subject + Verb + Complement (ASVC)Normally, the weather is good. Occasionally, my dog gets restless.
Adjunct + Subject + Verb + Object (ASVO)Every year, we visit the orphanage. Last week, we celebrated Christmas.

Fragments and run-ons

Sentence fragments

A sentence fragment is a word, phrase or clause that is not grammatically complete: it lacks a subject, a verb, or a complete thought.

Fragments

“As soon as I saw her.”
“Because she forgot her umbrella.”

Repaired

“As soon as I saw her, I waved.”
“Because she forgot her umbrella, she got wet.”

Practice: which are fragments?
  • “Milan and Mithra.” — Fragment (no verb)
  • “At the end of the program.” — Fragment (phrase only)
  • “There are exactly twenty boxes.” — Complete sentence
  • “The hospital is overflowing with patients.” — Complete sentence
  • “When my parents were living in New Delhi.” — Fragment (dependent clause)

Run-on sentences

A run-on sentence joins two or more independent clauses without correct punctuation or a conjunction. A comma splice joins them with only a comma.

Run-on / comma splice

“The students finished the layout they submitted it to the teacher.”
“She loves skiing, he doesn’t.”

Repaired

“The students finished the layout. They submitted it to the teacher.”
“She loves skiing, but he doesn’t.”

Parallelism

Parallelism uses matching sentence structure, phrases or longer parts to balance ideas of equal importance.

  1. Rule 1. Items in a list inside a sentence must have the same form. I had grilled steak, mashed potatoes and stir-fried vegetables for breakfast.
  2. Rule 2. Points in a list must have the same structure. In a procedure, begin every step with a command verb: Heat the pan. Add the mustard seeds. Stir the onions.
  3. Rule 3. A series of actions uses the same verb form. Rahul loves watching horror movies and playing video games. Similar or contrasting phrases share the same structure: Setting a timetable, studying your daily lessons and keeping track of your progress will help you achieve good grades.
Not parallel

“She went to the store for bread, milk, and to get some eggs.”

Parallel

“She went to the store for bread, milk, and eggs.”

Not parallel

“My favorite activities are reading books, playing video games, and to hike in the mountains.”

Parallel

“My favorite activities are reading books, playing video games, and hiking in the mountains.”

Not parallel

“The recipe calls for mixing the flour, sugar, and you should add the baking powder.”

Parallel

“The recipe calls for mixing the flour, sugar, and baking powder.”

Misplaced modifiers

A misplaced modifier sits too far from the word it describes, making the sentence awkward or accidentally funny. Place the modifier next to the word it modifies.

Misplaced

“She wore a bicycle helmet on her head that was too large.”

Corrected

“She wore a bicycle helmet that was too large on her head.”

Misplaced

“The patient was referred to the physician with stomach pains.”

Corrected

“The patient with stomach pains was referred to the physician.”

Misplaced

“Rolling down the mountain, the explorer stopped the boulder with his powerful foot.”

Corrected

“The explorer stopped the boulder rolling down the mountain with his powerful foot.”

Subject-verb agreement

Table 11. Subject-verb agreement rules
RuleExample
Compound subjects joined by and take a plural verb.Caren, Sheela and Akash have completed their assessments.
Singular nouns joined by or take a singular verb.Celery or spring onion works fine. Your mom or dad has to be here in an hour.
Anybody, anyone, no one, somebody, someone, everybody, everyone, nothing and nobody are singular.Everyone is happy with the outcome. Nothing fits me well.
With either…or and neither…nor, the verb agrees with the noun closest to it.Either the teachers or the student has to keep the classroom clean. Neither the children nor their parent is aware of the consequences.
Abstract and uncountable nouns are singular.Love makes people do crazy things. Honesty is the best policy.
A period of time, a distance or a sum of money takes a singular verb.1,267 kilometers is too far to travel in half a day.
Wishes and requests use the subjunctive were.I wish I were a bird. If you were here, I would not be sad.

Choosing words

Words are the building blocks of communication. In technical writing the right word shares knowledge, influences decisions and solves problems; the wrong word confuses or offends. Avoid these six kinds of language:

Table 12. Types of language to avoid
AvoidWhyBefore → after
JargonUse technical jargon only when readers are in the trade and will understand it.“The substance has a high vitriolic base.” → “The substance is strongly acidic.”
Double talkEmpty language that fills space. It suggests the writer lacks clarity or wants to mislead.“The impact of the restructuring at the present time is still undetermined.” → “We do not yet know how the restructuring will affect you. We will tell you as soon as we do.”
Sexist languageIt is disrespectful. Use gender-neutral terms or restructure the sentence.“A driver needs eight hours of sleep after he has driven for eight hours.” → “Drivers need eight hours of sleep after driving for eight hours.”
Biased languageDo not show a personal preference. Use objective language.“The car is expensive.” → “The car costs $60,000.”
Negative toneNegative words can alienate readers and disrupt good relations.“I will not process the order.” → “I will process your order as soon as I receive the signed form.”
ColloquialismsWords specific to a region may confuse other readers.“Leave the package on the stoop.” → “Leave the package at the front door.”

Parts of a Letter

By the end of this lecture you can
  • name and place the nine parts of a business letter;
  • compare full block, modified block and semi-block formats;
  • choose a suitable type of business letter; and
  • review a letter with a simple checklist before sending it.

Why letters still matter

A business letter is a formal, written message sent to someone outside your organization (inside it, you would send a memo). Employers, offices and clients still use letters for applications, requests, complaints, confirmations and recommendations because a letter creates a clear, professional, permanent record. A letter that is correctly formatted also shows that the writer respects the reader (the Dignity and Normal procedure of the ABCs).

The nine parts of a business letter

Writing the body well

Apply what you learned in the earlier lectures:

  • Lead with the purpose. “I am writing to request…” tells the reader immediately what to expect.
  • Use the you-point. Focus on what the reader needs to know or do.
  • Keep a positive, courteous tone. Avoid blame (“Because you did not…”). Say what you will do or what the reader can do.
  • Be specific. Give dates, amounts and reference numbers in the correct numerical style.
  • End with the action. State what you want, by when, and how to reach you.
Negative and vague

“You never answered my earlier message so I guess nobody cares about my problem.”

Positive and specific

“I sent a request on October 2 and have not yet received a reply. Could you please confirm the status of my request by October 17?”

Before you send: a checklist

  • Are all nine parts present and in the correct order?
  • Is the recipient’s name and title spelled correctly?
  • Does the first sentence state the purpose?
  • Is the tone courteous, positive and objective?
  • Are dates, amounts and addresses written consistently?
  • Is the letter free of spelling and grammar errors?
  • Did you sign it and list every enclosure?

Graphs and Charts

By the end of this lecture you can
  • explain why and how graphic aids are used in technical reports;
  • choose the right graph, chart, diagram or flowchart for your data; and
  • label every visual with a type, a number and a caption.

What are graphic aids?

Graphic aids (visual aids or technical illustrations) are non-textual elements that supplement written and spoken information. They make technical reports easier to navigate by translating abstract, dense or numerical data into clear visual patterns.

Three rules for every visual
  1. Place it well. A visual appears immediately after or near the text that explains it.
  2. Make it earn its place. Never include graphics purely for decoration.
  3. Label it. Give each visual a type (Figure or Table), a sequential number (Figure 1) and a descriptive caption.

Three major categories

Structural

Diagrams and flowcharts. Illustrate multi-step processes, systems, hierarchies and algorithms.

Quantitative

Graphs and charts. Represent numerical datasets, trends, frequencies and statistical relationships (bar, line, pie).

Illustrative

Pictures and posters. Convey bold, high-impact messages or realistic representations to win immediate attention.

Charts and graphs: the parts

A chart or graph presents numerical quantities. A complete one has these elements:

  • Scales (the numbers on each axis)
  • Grid (guide lines)
  • Title
  • Caption
  • Source reference
  • Labels or key

All data in this lecture’s charts is sample data invented for teaching; in your own reports, cite the real source.

Line graph

A line graph is useful for showing continuous relationships and trends, usually over time or distance. Use it when the question is “how does this change?”

Bar graph

A bar graph uses rectangular bars to display and compare discrete categories. The length or height of each bar is directly proportional to the value it represents. Always start the scale at zero.

Pie chart

A pie chart is a circle divided into slices that show numerical proportions. The whole circle represents 100% of the data; each slice’s angle and area correspond to its share. Use it for four or five parts at most.

Table

A table arranges words or figures in two or more columns. Use it when readers need exact values or when the relationships among different data matter as a whole.

Surface strata (stacked area) chart

A surface strata chart is similar to a line or bar graph, except that the area between the curve and the zero line is shaded. Its features:

  • Layered composition: components are stacked vertically rather than side by side.
  • Continuous area fill: each layer has its own color, pattern or shading.
  • Double readability: readers can follow both the macro trend (the top line, the total) and the micro trends (each layer’s size).

Pictograph

A pictograph uses small pictures or icons to represent quantities. A key tells the reader how much one icon is worth. It is friendly and quick to read, which makes it good for general audiences, but it is less precise than a bar graph.

Map charts

Map charts (geographic or spatial charts) show numerical or categorical data across geographical boundaries. Common kinds:

  • Choropleth (filled) maps: color gradients show values across regions such as countries, provinces or zip codes (for example, population density).
  • Bubble or scatter maps: bubbles on locations; size or color shows magnitude (for example, store sales per city).
  • Heat maps: continuous color gradients show areas of high or low density (for example, foot traffic or crime rates).
  • Pie-chart maps: small pie charts placed on coordinates show category shares per region.

Diagrams

A diagram explains how something works, what it is made of, or how steps relate. It combines two kinds of elements:

  • Structural elements: lines, arrows, shapes (boxes, circles) and text labels that show relationships, flow or hierarchy.
  • Pictorial elements: simple illustrations or icons of physical objects, such as an organ, a machine part or a building component.

Flowcharts

Flowcharts translate complex, multi-step processes into clear, scannable visual paths. They are used for:

  • Sequencing
  • Decision mapping
  • Troubleshooting
  • Process standardization

Rounded boxes mark the start and end, rectangles mark actions, and diamonds mark decisions with “Yes” and “No” branches.
